### Video Games
#### (I) - What is a video game?
A video game can generally be defined as an electronic interactive game that uses a visual display of some kind to convey the events and/or goal of the game. Usually, this takes the form of a piece of software that listens for player inputs and streams the contents of the game to a video screen, typically with audio to go alongside it, though it's possible for games to be implemented using hardware instead, such as Pong. 

When the term was coined, the definition revolved more around hardware due to the technology being brand new, but in the modern day video games are now simply a "type" of game, and we don't think about the video element as much now that video is everywhere. 
#### (II) - What is considered the first video game? and What are the console wars?
It is difficult to say definitively what the "first video game" truly was, as the technology was still in its infancy, and there are several candidates that either do or don't count based on the way one defines "video game"; on top of that, nobody was really keeping track of "who made the first video game" as the concept didn't really exist yet, so it's a lot of tech demos and "almost-video" games. Some possible answers include the electronic arcade game Bertie the Brain, built in 1950 by Josef Kates, which used lightbulbs to display a player-vs-CPU game of tic-tac-toe, or Tennis for Two, created by William Higinbotham in 1958, which was a tennis game that used an oscilloscope as a display and which was possibly the first of its kind to be created simply for entertainment and not as a tech demo. 

#### (III) - What is a video game genre? What different genres are there?
A 'genre' as it pertains to video games, rather than describing the setting or narrative style as they do with other media such as film and literature, most commonly refer to the style of gameplay. For instance, a gothic horror inspired mystery game can be considered the same genre as a slice-of-life cosy game, so long as both games play like an RPG. I've described a few game genres below, as well as others [here](https://haruzova.github.io/eob-pf/LV2-Units/202-Digital-Games-Production/#iii-describing-the-characteristics-of-four-different-game-genres-and-giving-examples-of-each).

**Puzzle** games are, as the name suggests, games with a heavy emphasis on puzzle-solving gameplay. This can mean puzzle-platformers such as Portal, or a pure-puzzle game like Minesweeper, and the puzzles could come in a discrete level-based format, or be a part of a larger environment/world.

**Horror** is somewhat of an outlier here in that despite being a game genre, it doesn't actually refer to the mechanics in particular and is used more like the narrative genre of horror. Horror games can have all kinds of gameplay, from Squirrel Stapler's active hunting gameplay loop to Five Nights at Freddy's sit-and-survive core, so long as their narrative, visuals, atmosphere or general design still lend themselves towards a horror theme or scaring the player.

**RTS**, aka Real-Time Strategy games are a type of strategy game where the player actions and events happen in real-time, as opposed to through a turn-based or action value system. This can look like a tower-defense system such as in Bloons TD where the player has to build up defenses quickly, city-building and management like in Frostpunk, or exploration and dungeon crawling like in Pikmin 2, with the common elements that define the genre being a focus on strategic time and resource management.

There are both a lot of stereotypes, and a wide variety of demographics when it cokmes to who plays games, so this is difficult to describe perfectly. Some would say that gamers are mostly young children or teens—particularly boys—due to how video games were once advertised, but it's becoming increasingly clear that this isn't the case anymore. 

 According to X, 38% of gamers are aged 18-34 compared to under 18s at 20% as the second largest age bracket [1](https://www.theesa.com/resource/2021-essential-facts-about-the-video-game-industry/), and the gender split is balanced, at 52% male to 47% female (and 1% other) [2](https://www.statista.com/statistics/232383/gender-split-of-us-computer-and-video-gamers/) as of 2025. Compared to 2006's 62/38 gender split, the gap has closed somewhat, though even that older figure doesn't match up to the stereotype. 

 Most gamers appear to be using mobile platforms, with 34% stating they play on a mobile phone and 14% on a tablet, contrasted with games consoles coming in at 24%, laptops at 12% and PCs at 11% [3](https://www.statista.com/statistics/260531/devices-used-to-play-games-in-the-uk/) as of 2024. 

### Game Creation

#### (I) - What is game design?
Game design refers to the process of designing and building the various components of a game, like the objective, systems and rules, and mechanics; with video games, this also includes things such as the environments/world, story, and difficulty balance. From the GDD and story drafts, to UI and UX, the readability of enemies and hazards, level layouts and tutorials, accessibility integration... game design covers almost everything that has to go into creating a game. 

#### (II) - What are the first steps in designing a game?
The first steps of designing a game can look different from project to project, depending on scale, game type, engine, and who's developing is, but here is how it *might* look for a project.

If starting in the pre-production stage of game development, some of the first steps would be gathering ideas for the game and building a GDD (games design document) for the project, including things such as the genre, potential gameplay loops, visual style,theme/story, character examples or ideas, and setting. Deciding on a game engine (if any) and scope might also happen here.

During early stages of *production*, you might start with building a basic blockout environment and the player representation/character along with their basic movement or other primary mechanic. The assets can be placeholders at this stage, and the scripts can be basic; the purpose is just getting something resembling a game into existence to build on top of. You could then work on designing level layouts, start to think about adding in other entities such as enemies, or make a start on the core game mechanic such as jumping or moving objects.

#### (V) - What game engines can a developer use to create a game?
There are a lot of different engines that all have their advantages and weaknesses for a given developer or project, like Godot, Unity, Unreal Engine, RPGMaker, RenPy, etc. Some game studios have their own in-house game engines with their own specialisations for the style, genre, or features their games tend to need.

#### (VI) - What factors would a dev need to consider when developing a game for mobile, console and VR?
When developing for different platforms, you might have to consider factors such as control schemes, compatibility and performance on different devices, and the unique UX needs of the target platform. On a standard games console it's mostly a matter of making sure the game is optimised for each particular console it's being released on and plays well with the given controller for that console, but other platforms aren't always as straightforward.

Mobile complicates things by requiring touchscreen input and not inherently having a controller or a keyboard available, so the UI of your game has to both be readable on a smaller screen and make room for on-screen controls (whether visible or not), and performance becomes a bigger concern. With so much variety in mobile devices and their performance, you need to take a PC-like approach—optimising and ensuring compatibility with more than one device configuration and OS, considering minimum and target device specs, and including some graphics adjustment options so that users can better tailor how the game runs to their needs. 

VR is somewhere between PC/Mobile and console in terms of hardware variation; there are some VR consoles and there are users that play VR through a PC setup, so depending on where a VR game releases on the performance and compatibility needs can vary. The actual main factor a dev needs to consider for releasing on VR though is that of playability. A VR game needs to have a control scheme and visuals, or at least options for those, that will minimise the negative physical effects of using VR and not cause the players discomfort. Avoiding intense camera movement that could be disorienting would be a common example, as well as making sure it's possible to take regular breaks from the game via adding a lot of checkpoints or keeping gameplay to small levels/rounds.
